[0:12 — SCREEN: Switch to Slide 3 — Problem Analysis]

P:
Every factory is divided into stations where each step of the process happens — the product moves along a conveyor belt from one to the next, much like the food manufacturing videos you may have seen online or on TV.

Each station has a target pace it needs to hold — called a takt time — and as long as everyone keeps up, the whole line flows.

The problem starts the moment one station doesn't.
When Station 4 runs just two minutes slow — a worn tool, a drifting
process parameter, anything imaginable or unimaginable — something very predictable happens.

Stations behind it keep finishing on time. Cars pile up, waiting.
Stations ahead of it run out of work entirely and sit idle.

One local delay. Queue on one side, starvation on the other.
That's the ripple.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

[0:42 — still Slide 3]

M: The other thing is — bottlenecks don't sit still. The station that slows everything down on Monday might be completely fine on Tuesday, 
just because they're building a different vehicle model that day. So a report at the end of a shift is always telling you what already happened. 
It can't help with what's happening right now.

Then there's the quality side of it. There's a well-known principle in manufacturing called the 1:10:100 rule. It basically says — the later you catch a mistake, the more expensive it gets. 
Catch it right where it happened, quick fix, low cost. Catch it at the end of the line after the car is mostly assembled — 
now you might have to take things apart just to get to the problem. 
And if it reaches the customer, you're looking at warranty claims, potentially a recall. 
The cost multiplies at every stage it goes undetected.

What makes this worse is that a lot of older factory stations don't have much digital monitoring on them at all. 
So the places where problems are most likely to slip through are also the places nobody's properly watching.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

[1:15 — SCREEN: Switch to Slide 4 — Proposed Solution]

P:
the first thing we want to say is we are NOT building.

what i mean by that is we are not building a full 3D replica of the factory floor.
Because geometry doesn't predict anything. Rhythm on the belt does.

We call it the Assembly Intelligence Layer — three components, working as one.

The first is the Line Mirror. Not a photograph of the factory — a live
functional model tracking cycle time versus takt time per station,
queue depth between stations, and each vehicle's process history
as it moves down the line.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

[1:45 — still Slide 4]

P:
The second is the Flow Brain.
It learns what normal looks like for each station from historical data.
Then it watches for drift — not a hard alarm, but a gradual-creep detector
that catches a station trending off before it becomes a failure.

And it doesn't just flag it. It forecasts the consequence.
"Station 5's buffer will empty in approximately 18 minutes."
That's a warning you can act on — not a siren after the damage is done.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

[2:10 — still Slide 4]

M:
The third is the Defect Radar.
Every vehicle on the line carries a rolling risk score — built from the
parameters it passed through at each station.
If Station 4 was drifting while your car went through it, that car's score
goes up. When it crosses a threshold, it gets flagged for a targeted check
at the next inspection gate.

Not at the end of the line.
That's the 1:10:100 rule applied in reverse —
catching at cost one, not cost ten or cost a hundred.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

[2:35 — still Slide 4, bottom section]

P:
And for stations with no sensors — which is a real, common problem
in older factories — we have three approaches.

Neighbour inference — watch the stations on either side to estimate
what the middle one is doing.

Soft sensors — power draw, a basic camera feed, or a single
accelerometer can stand in for a dedicated process sensor.

And uncertainty modeling — instead of ignoring the gap, the system
shows the probability. "68% likely normal, 32% running slow."
Honest and actionable, instead of confidently wrong.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

P:
A comparable real-time bottleneck detection system improved
Overall Equipment Effectiveness by over 30% in a year-long
automotive powertrain pilot. That number is from published research.
It's not theoretical.

M:
This was our approach to solve problem 4. Thank you for listening.
